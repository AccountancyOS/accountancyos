The short answer: **before you need it**. Effective tax planning for an owner-managed
company is a habit of looking ahead across the year — and starting well before any
significant transaction — rather than a task saved for the weeks around a filing
deadline.

This piece is general information for company owners and directors. It is not advice for
your specific circumstances, and tax rules and rates change; always confirm the current
position and take advice before acting.

## Why timing matters more than technique

Most of the tax decisions that genuinely move the needle for an owner-managed company
share one feature: they have to be made *before* something happens. Once a dividend is
paid, an asset is bought, or a company year end has passed, the range of sensible options
narrows considerably.

Planning early does not mean doing anything artificial. It means giving yourself enough
time to understand the position, weigh the realistic options and choose deliberately.

## The points in the year that reward looking ahead

For most companies, a handful of recurring moments are worth anticipating:

- **Before the company year end** — the year end fixes the profit on which Corporation
  Tax is assessed. Decisions about timing of income, expenditure and capital purchases are
  only available beforehand.
- **When taking money out of the company** — the balance between salary and dividends, and
  the level of dividends against available reserves, is a decision to plan, not to
  discover afterwards.
- **Before significant expenditure** — the tax treatment of capital purchases depends on
  the rules in force and how a purchase is structured and timed.
- **Before a transaction or change in structure** — a sale, a reorganisation or bringing
  in a new shareholder can carry consequences that are difficult to unwind later.

## A simple discipline

Owner-managed companies rarely need constant, elaborate planning. They benefit from a
predictable rhythm:

1. Keep a reliable view of the likely profit and tax position through the year, rather than
   waiting for the year-end accounts.
2. Look ahead to the next year end and any known decisions before they arrive.
3. Raise anything significant — a purchase, a transaction, a change in how profits are
   drawn — as a question in advance, not a report after the event.

That rhythm is usually enough to avoid the most common and most avoidable problem: finding
out what could have been done only once it is too late to do it.

## What this is not

This is not a case for chasing every possible saving. A transaction should stand on its
commercial merits first; the tax treatment is one input among several. Good planning
weighs the tax position within the wider objective — and is candid when the sensible course
is to accept a tax cost in exchange for a better outcome.
